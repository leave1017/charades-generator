Halloween Charades Party Guide: Rules, Teams & 40 Words That Work

🎭

Written by the Charades Generator team. We build the free word generator this site runs on. The last time we went through the word bank by hand we cut sixty-three entries — words that read perfectly well on a list and died the moment somebody actually had to act one out. That is the bar everything here is held to.

Halloween charades looks like ordinary charades with spookier words on the cards. It is not. The room is darker, half the players are wearing something that covers their face or their hands, the children have eaten a remarkable amount of sugar, and the age range is wider than at any other party of the year. Those four things break the game in ways a normal word list never has to deal with. This guide is about the parts nobody writes down: how to pick teams, how long a turn should be, what to do when the room stalls, and which words are worth putting on a card in the first place.

If you just want words on screen and nothing else, the Halloween Charades Generator draws a random one every tap, and the printable card builder turns a set into cards you can cut out before guests arrive. Everything below is what to do with them.

Five minutes of prep that decide the whole night

The single biggest predictor of whether charades works at a party is whether the words exist before people arrive. Passing a phone around means the person whose turn it is has to read a word, hide the screen, hand the phone off and then start acting — and every one of those steps is a moment for someone to see the answer. Physical cards remove all of it.

Count on six to eight words per player. Ten people playing for about an hour will burn through roughly seventy words, and you want spares so nobody draws something a previous team already acted out. Cut them the afternoon before, not while guests are taking their coats off.

You also need two things people forget: a visible timer and a referee. A phone timer face-down on the sofa is invisible to the actor, who then has no idea whether to keep refining a clue or switch tactics. Put the timer where the actor can see it. And appoint one person who draws the cards, calls time and settles arguments — usually whoever is hosting and drinking least. They do not play. A game with a referee runs about twice as many rounds per hour as one without.

Picking teams when the ages are all over the place

The obvious split — grown-ups against children — is the one that fails. It sounds fair and it is fun for about four minutes, until the adult team wins every round, the children lose interest, and the adults start throwing rounds so obviously that the children notice. Two lopsided teams is not a game.

Split the ages across teams instead. Line everyone up roughly by age and deal them out alternately, so each team ends up with a similar spread. The nine-year-old who cannot decode "Poltergeist" is on the same side as the aunt who can, and the aunt is the one shouting the guess — which is exactly the experience you want the nine-year-old to have.

Odd number of players? Do not leave someone out and do not make the teams uneven. Make the odd person the referee for the first half, then swap them in and pull someone else out at the halfway point. Refereeing is a real job, not a punishment.

Very large group? Above about fourteen players, stop making bigger teams and make more of them. Three or four teams of four is far better than two teams of eight, because in a team of eight there are always two people who never actually get a turn.

One Halloween-specific wrinkle: the costume handicap. Masks hide the face, which is where half of all charades information lives. Gloves and claws hide the hands. Long capes and hooped skirts make full-body mime clumsy. Ask people to take off masks and gloves for their own turn — you will get better acting and much better photographs.

How long a turn should actually be

Sixty seconds is the standard and it is the right default for a mixed adult and teenage room. Adjust from there rather than treating it as a rule:

Who is acting Turn length Why
Adults only 45 seconds Sixty feels slack; the pressure is most of the comedy
Mixed adults and teens 60 seconds The standard, and it needs no explaining
Children under eight 90 seconds Young children spend the first half deciding what to do
Classroom 60 seconds, hard stop Thirty children means turn count matters more than any single turn

Whatever you pick, stick to it. The version that ruins an evening is the one where the referee lets a turn run long because the actor is close — do that once and every future turn becomes a negotiation.

Three ways to score, for three kinds of room

Most people play one version of charades and assume that is the game. Scoring is the lever that makes it suit your particular room, and switching between rounds keeps a long evening from flattening out.

1. Classic — one point per correct guess

Teams alternate, one point per word guessed inside the time, first to fifteen wins. Use it when the group has not played together before. It is the version everyone already understands, so nobody spends the first ten minutes asking how it works.

2. Escalating stakes — easy 1, medium 2, hard 3

Sort the cards into three piles by difficulty and let the acting team choose which pile to draw from. Suddenly there is a decision to make before the turn even starts, and a team that is behind has a way back in. This is the best version for competitive adults, and it is the reason the words further down this page are grouped by difficulty rather than by theme.

3. Cooperative clock — everyone against the timer

No teams at all. Set ten minutes on the clock and see how many words the whole room can get through together. Play it again later in the evening and try to beat the number. This is the version for parties with small children, for groups where one person is much better at the game than everyone else, and for anyone who has watched charades turn into an argument.

A fourth option worth knowing: pass tokens. Give each team two passes for the whole game. A team that draws a word nobody can act may hand it back once, at the cost of a token. It removes the single most deflating moment in charades — a turn that dies in the first five seconds — without letting teams shop around for easy words.

40 Halloween words that actually work

A word earns its place on a card if there is something to do with it. Each of these comes with the angle that makes it playable — hand the list to whoever is refereeing, because the acting note is often the difference between a word that lands and a word that dies.

🟢 Easy — for younger players and opening rounds

Ghost — arms out, float, walk through a wall. The first word everyone reaches for, and it works.
Pumpkin — draw the round shape, then carve a face. The carving is what separates it from "ball".
Black Cat — four legs, then an arched back and a hiss. Point at something black first.
Witch — the pointed hat is the whole clue. Draw it in the air, then stir a cauldron.
Spider — eight fingers walking. Do not mime the web first or you will get "fishing net".
Skeleton — stiff limbs and a rattle. Tapping your own ribs reads instantly.
Bat — hang upside down if the furniture allows; flap if it does not.
Vampire — the cape sweep across the face, then the fangs. Two beats, in that order.
Trick or Treat — knock on a door, hold out a bag, look expectant. A three-part mime a six-year-old can do.
Candy — unwrap something small, eat it, be delighted. Easiest word on this list.
Mummy — wrap yourself, then walk stiffly with arms forward. The wrapping has to come first.
Broomstick — sweep, then straddle it and fly. Sweeping alone gets "cleaning".
Full Moon — draw the circle, point up, then howl. The howl is what confirms it.
Jack-o'-Lantern — pumpkin, then cup your hands around a candle inside it.

🟠 Medium — the core of most rounds

Haunted House — draw the roof, then react to something behind you. Two ideas, joined.
Werewolf — start human, transform, end on all fours. The transformation is the clue, not the wolf.
Zombie — the walk is universal, but hold it longer than feels natural before adding the reaching.
Graveyard — draw several headstone shapes in a row. One headstone is "grave"; a row is the word.
Cauldron — stir a big pot, then recoil from the fumes. The recoil is what stops it being "soup".
Scarecrow — arms out on an invisible pole and go completely still. Stillness is the whole performance.
Cobweb — walk into it, then spend five seconds pulling it off your face. Everyone recognises the second half.
Crystal Ball — cup both hands, stare in, react to what you see.
Bobbing for Apples — kneel, hands behind your back, face into an imaginary tub. Nothing else looks like it.
Costume Party — mime putting on a mask, then a second, then a third. Repetition signals "party".
Frankenstein's Monster — bolts at the neck first, then the stiff walk. Neck bolts are the identifier.
Candy Corn — draw the triangle, then the two stripes across it. A shape word, so draw it big.
Fortune Teller — crystal ball, then read a palm. Two props make the role clear.
Trick-or-Treaters — act out a small group moving door to door, holding hands at different heights.

🔴 Hard — for the escalating-stakes pile

Poltergeist — do not mime a ghost. Mime objects flying off shelves while you stand still and terrified.
Séance — sit, join hands with people who are not there, speak in a voice that is not yours.
Grim Reaper — pull up a hood, then swing a scythe. The scythe carries it.
Headless Horseman — lift your own head off, tuck it under your arm, then ride. Split it as three syllable-chunks if the room stalls.
Exorcism — hold up a cross, back away, then be the thing being backed away from. Switching roles mid-mime is the trick.
Vampire Hunter — sharpen a stake, then use it. Sequence matters or it reads as "carpenter".
Haunted Mirror — mime a frame, look into it, then react to a reflection that moves on its own.
Black Magic — a two-word clue. Point at something black, then pull a rabbit from a hat.
Shape-shifter — become three different creatures in quick succession. Speed is the clue, not any one creature.
Cursed Doll — cradle something, set it down, then have it turn its head towards you.
Midnight — point at a clock, hold up ten fingers then two, then react to something waking up.
Possessed — fight your own arm. One half of the body against the other reads instantly once it starts.

Words to leave off the cards

Cutting the wrong words matters more than adding good ones, because a single dead card can flatten the room for a full minute. Three kinds to strike:

  • ✕ Abstract nouns. Fear, superstition, the supernatural, evil. There is no gesture for them. They look atmospheric on a list and they are unactable in a living room.
  • ✕ Names only half the room knows. A slasher villain from 1984 is a great card at a party of film fans and a dead card everywhere else. The test is not whether the word is famous, it is whether the guessing team knows it.
  • ✕ Anything genuinely frightening, if small children are playing. One scared six-year-old ends the game for everyone in the room, and no card is worth that.

There is a fourth, subtler one: words that are too close together. Ghost, spirit, phantom and apparition all get mimed the same way, so a deck with all four produces three rounds that feel like reruns. Pick one and spend the other three slots elsewhere.

The same game in three different rooms

A living-room party, eight to twelve people

Two teams, alternating, sixty seconds, first to fifteen. Clear a proper acting space before you start — charades dies quietly when the actor has nowhere to stand and ends up performing in a two-foot gap between the coffee table and the sofa. Move the table.

A classroom, twenty-five to thirty children

Everything changes at this size, because the real constraint is how many children get a turn. Use four teams rather than two, keep turns to a hard sixty seconds, and hand out the cards face-down in advance so there is no queue at the front of the room. Easy words only — a class of thirty has one child who will remember for the rest of term that they were the one who could not do theirs. Our classroom charades guide goes through the group management in more detail.

An adults-only party

Shorten turns to forty-five seconds, use the escalating-stakes scoring, and put the hard pile within reach. If people have been drinking, drop the rule that the actor cannot make any sound at all — strict silence is exactly the rule a loosened-up room cannot keep, and enforcing it turns the referee into a traffic warden. Let people make noises that are not words, and the game gets funnier rather than more chaotic.

Five ways it goes wrong, and what to do

The same two people act every round.

Rotate in a fixed order rather than asking for volunteers. Nobody volunteers into a room where the confident people already went.

Rounds keep ending with nobody guessing.

Your words are too hard for the room, not your players too slow. Move the whole deck one difficulty tier down and the energy comes back within two turns.

Arguments about whether a guess counted.

Agree before the first turn that close counts — "Frankenstein" for "Frankenstein's Monster" is a point. The referee's call is final and is made in one second, not debated.

Children lose interest after fifteen minutes.

Fifteen minutes is about right for under-eights. Stop while it is still fun and come back after food. A game that ends on a high gets asked for again.

One team is twenty points ahead.

Do not let it grind out. Switch to the cooperative clock for the rest of the evening — the score stops mattering and everyone keeps playing.

Common questions

How many words do I need?

Six to eight per player. Ten people playing for an hour get through about seventy, and spares stop teams drawing a word that has already been acted out.

Can the actor make sounds?

Traditionally no. In practice, decide before the first turn and stick to it. Silent is a better game; sounds-allowed is a better party. Do not change the rule halfway through, which is what causes the arguments.

Should I mix Halloween words with ordinary ones?

For an hour-long game, yes. A pure Halloween deck starts to repeat itself — there are only so many ways to mime a ghost. Roughly two Halloween words to one ordinary word keeps the theme while giving the room some variety. The main generator lets you tick both.

What about players who refuse to act?

Give them the referee job or make them the official scorekeeper. Everyone at a party should have a role in the game; not everyone has to be in front of it.

The short version

Cut the cards the day before. Split the ages across teams instead of against each other. Put the timer where the actor can see it, give one person the job of refereeing, and take the masks off for your own turn. Pick the scoring that fits the room, and change it when the room changes. Everything else is words on cards — and words are the easy part.
